How it works

Three steps,
zero new sensors.

The meters are already there and already reporting voltage every fifteen minutes for billing. That data carries the shape of the network. It has simply never been read that way.

01

Strip the noise that hides the network

Raw meter voltage is dominated by the substation's own swing, which every consumer sees at once. Working on voltage increments and subtracting the cross-sectional median removes that shared component. What survives is the local drop across the impedance a meter shares with its transformer. The topology signal.

02

Let the network cluster itself

Meters on the same phase conductor share its voltage drop almost exactly, so they correlate at 0.81 while unrelated meters sit near zero. Clustering recovers the transformer-and-phase groups perfectly; matching each group against busbar telemetry labels it with a real asset ID. Usable after two days of data.

03

Read condition, and faults, off the same signal

With a correct map, the physics becomes legible: estimated impedance per transformer and per segment, tracked against each asset's own baseline. Rising neutral impedance is a corroding joint. A last-gasp burst is a located fault. Midday overvoltage with export is reverse power flow. Answered with reactive setpoints before curtailment.

04

Turn the map into flexibility

With connectivity known, every phase of every transformer gets a day ahead net load forecast, built from calendar position, its own recent history and a purchased irradiance forecast that is deliberately degraded to the accuracy a utility would really buy. Where the forecast breaches a thermal or voltage limit, the system picks the consumers who can actually help, on the phase that is actually in trouble, and sizes storage for whatever demand response cannot cover.

What is inside

Engineering,
not a black box.

Every output is an impedance in ohms, a named consumer, or a bracketed span of line. A distribution engineer can argue with all of it. Which is the only way a control room ever comes to trust it.

Physics

Four-wire power flow

Backward/forward sweep solving all three phases plus neutral, exact for radial LV and stable at the high R/X ratios where Newton-Raphson struggles.

Topology

Self-learning connectivity

Meter → transformer → phase inferred from voltage correlation, with a confidence score and an explicit field-check list for the cases it is unsure about.

Health

Per-asset baselines

No fleet-wide thresholds. A 63 kVA rural unit and a 250 kVA urban one are judged against themselves, so neither is missed nor cried wolf over.

Health

Segment-level detection

A bad joint with three consumers behind it moves the transformer average by nothing. A second pass at meter level catches it and names the group.

Outage

Depth-bracketed faults

Electrical depth estimated per consumer, so a fault is bounded between the deepest lit meter and the shallowest dark one. In ohms from the transformer.

Solar

Reverse-flow separation

Overvoltage coincident with PV export is distinguished from load-driven sag, and vars are allocated to the deepest inverters where they buy the most volts.

Honesty

Unassessable is reported

Indicators whose noise exceeds their signal are surfaced as unmeasurable, never quietly passed as healthy. Silent gaps are how monitoring loses trust.

Edge

Built for 2G backhaul

Inference belongs at the DT gateway with anomalies uplinked, not a firehose to the cloud. Because rural comms will not carry a firehose.

Forecast

Per phase, not per transformer

A feeder is rarely in trouble as a whole. One phase overloads while another idles, and a forecast that sums the three together cannot see the problem worth acting on.

Flexibility

Deferrable load inferred, not asked

Nobody knows which consumers can shift load. Pumping looks unmistakable in interval data: long runs, high peaks, mostly at night. Scored from shape alone, never from a tariff category.

Storage

Sized from the forecast

Power set by the largest predicted deficit, energy by the largest deficit area, charging from the midday export the same feeder is already spilling.

Integration

Feeds ADMS and DERMS

Designed as the low voltage layer beneath an existing EcoStruxure Grid, ADMS or DERMS deployment. Both assume a network model that Indian LV does not have.

Evidence

This is a hackathon entry, so it is validated the only honest way available: against a simulated network whose true connectivity, degradation and faults are known exactly. And which no model is allowed to read.

The utility ledger had 150 consumers on the wrong transformer or phase. ENTITY GRID found every one of them from voltage data alone, without a single site visit. The obvious method, plain correlation, scores below the ledger it was replacing.

Ground truth is used only for scoring; a test in the suite fails the build if the topology learner so much as opens a truth file. Questions we expect

The hard ones,
answered first.

Does this need new meters, sensors or gateways?
No. It consumes interval voltage and current that RDSS-compliant meters already report for billing, plus the transformer-level telemetry those programmes already mandate. The entire contribution is software. That is the point. Anything requiring field hardware dies on capex approval.
How is this different from an outage management system we could buy?
Conventional OMS assumes an accurate network model and instrumented MV feeders. Indian LV networks have neither. Which is why those deployments underperform here. ENTITY GRID treats topology as something to be learned rather than assumed, and is built to feed an existing ADMS rather than replace it.
Is 100% connectivity accuracy realistic?
On this simulation, yes; in the field, expect less. The number is real but it comes from synthetic data, and we say so everywhere it appears. What travels to the field is the method and its degradation curve: still 99% with 15% of reads missing, falling to 88% at 30% missing, and 97% on a single day of data. Those numbers matter more than the headline.
What happens when meters do not report?
Short gaps are interpolated, longer ones left as gaps and excluded from the correlation rather than invented. Fault recall is explicitly capped by the roughly 14% of last-gasp messages lost to RF collisions during a mass outage. Precision stays at 100%, because a meter that reported dark really was dark.
Can it tell a failing transformer from a merely overloaded one?
Yes, because it tracks impedance, not load. Overload raises current and drops voltage without changing the ohms between busbar and consumer. Degradation raises those ohms. Separate estimates are kept for the transformer winding, the phase conductor and the neutral, and the three fail in different ways.
How does this answer a challenge about intermittency and storage?
By making the rest of it aimable. Forecasting, demand response and shared storage all need to know which consumers sit behind which transformer and phase, and that is exactly the record that is wrong a third of the time. ENTITY GRID recovers it, then builds the flexibility layer on top: per phase day ahead forecasts at 13% better than the best free baseline, predicted constraint windows, call lists filtered to the phase in trouble, and storage sized from the forecast.
Did anything you tried not work?
Two things, both still in the repository. A cumulative sum control chart is the textbook detector for slow degradation and is what the published work uses; on this data it raised eight false alarms to catch one real fault, against zero false alarms for the trend test that ships. Restricting correlation to low load intervals, which the literature reports as a clear improvement, gained about three points under clock drift and lost ground everywhere else, so it is available and off by default. Both comparisons are on the Evidence tab of the console.
Where does it admit it cannot help?
Busbar-referenced winding detection fails on a feeder whose MV source drifts. The reference moves as much as the asset. So those indicators are reported as unassessable rather than passed as healthy. Feeder-wide faults sitting right at the busbar defeat the peer-comparison detector, which is why the transformer-level neutral indicator exists alongside it. Both limits are visible in the console.
